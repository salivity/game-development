Computing Vehicle Mass and Inertia Tensors in Games

Accurately simulating vehicle dynamics in video games requires calculating realistic mass distributions and moment-of-inertia tensors for compound assemblies. Developers achieve this by decomposing a vehicle into discrete sub-components, computing their individual mass properties and center of mass, shifting their local inertia tensors to a common reference frame using the parallel axis theorem, and aggregating the results into a single rigid-body tensor. This workflow guarantees that behaviors like body roll, pitch under braking, and mid-air tumbling react convincingly to physical forces while remaining computationally efficient for real-time physics engines.

1. Component Decomposition and Local Inertia

A vehicle is rarely treated as a single uniform solid. Instead, developers break the assembly into distinct logical components such as the chassis frame, engine block, transmission, fuel tank, battery, and passengers. Each component is assigned an estimated or real-world mass and approximated using geometric primitives (boxes, cylinders, spheres) or simplified convex collision hulls.

For standard primitives, closed-form analytic formulas directly yield the local moment-of-inertia tensor (\(I_{local}\)) relative to the component's own center of mass. For complex arbitrary meshes, developers often use numerical integration, such as Brian Mirtich’s divergence theorem algorithm, to compute exact volume and inertia properties based on the assumption of uniform density across the mesh.

2. Computing the Compound Center of Mass

Before combining rotational properties, the unified Center of Mass (CoM) of the entire vehicle must be established. The compound CoM is calculated as the mass-weighted average position of all individual sub-components:

\[\mathbf{R}_{\text{CoM}} = \frac{\sum m_i \mathbf{r}_i}{\sum m_i}\]

Where \(m_i\) is the mass of component \(i\) and \(\mathbf{r}_i\) is the position of its local center of mass in vehicle-space coordinates. Determining this point is crucial, as any offset directly shifts the vehicle’s pitch, roll, and yaw pivots.

3. Applying the Parallel Axis Theorem

Individual inertia tensors are defined around their own local centers of mass. To sum them, each tensor must be shifted to the newly calculated vehicle CoM. Developers accomplish this using the multi-dimensional Parallel Axis Theorem (also known as Steiner’s Theorem).

For a component with mass \(m\) and an offset vector \(\mathbf{d} = (d_x, d_y, d_z)\) from its local center of mass to the vehicle's compound CoM, the parallel axis translation adds an offset matrix:

\[I_{\text{offset}} = m \begin{bmatrix} d_y^2 + d_z^2 & -d_x d_y & -d_x d_z \\ -d_x d_y & d_x^2 + d_z^2 & -d_y d_z \\ -d_x d_z & -d_y d_z & d_x^2 + d_y^2 \end{bmatrix}\]

If the sub-component is rotated relative to the vehicle's coordinate frame, its local tensor \(I_{\text{local}}\) is first rotated using its orientation matrix \(R\) (\(R I_{\text{local}} R^T\)) before adding the offset matrix.

4. Tensor Aggregation and Diagonalization

The complete vehicle inertia tensor is the direct matrix sum of the transformed inertia tensors of all individual components:

\[I_{\text{total}} = \sum \left( R_i I_{\text{local}, i} R_i^T + I_{\text{offset}, i} \right)\]

This yields a symmetric \(3 \times 3\) matrix representing the combined resistance to rotational acceleration. Most standard physics engines (such as PhysX, Havok, or Jolt) require this tensor to be diagonalized to find the vehicle's principal axes of inertia. Eigenvalue decomposition is performed on \(I_{\text{total}}\):

  • The eigenvalues yield the principal moments of inertia (stored as a vector: \(I_{xx}\), \(I_{yy}\), \(I_{zz}\)).
  • The eigenvectors define the principal rotation frame relative to the vehicle body.

5. Practical Tuning and Gameplay Adjustments

While raw mathematical calculation provides physical accuracy, it does not always yield enjoyable gameplay. Developers frequently modify the calculated values:

  • Lowering the Center of Mass: The vertical position of the CoM is often artificially lowered below the physical axles to prevent excessive rolling or flipping during sharp turns.
  • Manipulating Yaw Inertia: Increasing the principal moment along the vertical axis (\(I_{zz}\)) makes the vehicle feel heavier and less prone to erratic, hyper-sensitive spinning.
  • Separating Sprung vs. Unsprung Mass: Suspension assemblies, wheels, and brakes are often decoupled from the main compound body and simulated as dynamic constraints to ensure that wheel impacts do not unrealistically jerk the chassis.
