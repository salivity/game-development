Dual Quaternion Skinning for Twist Joints in Games

Dual Quaternion Skinning (DQS) is an advanced character deformation technique in game development designed to eliminate the severe volume loss and "candy-wrapper" collapsing artifacts caused by traditional Linear Blend Skinning (LBS). When skeletal joints undergo extreme twisting—such as wrists or shoulders rotating up to 180 degrees—traditional skinning methods mistakenly scale down geometry, pinching the mesh inward. By representing both rotation and translation within a unified mathematical framework of dual quaternions, game engines can interpolate transformations along the shortest curved path, preserving mesh volume and maintaining structural fidelity in real time.

The Failure of Linear Blend Skinning (LBS)

In standard Linear Blend Skinning, vertex transformations are calculated by taking a weighted linear average of transformation matrices:

\[v' = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i M_i v\]

While computationally cheap and easy to compute on the GPU, \(4 \times 4\) transformation matrices do not form a linear space. When interpolating between two matrices that have opposite or opposing rotational orientations—such as a forearm bone twisting 180 degrees relative to the elbow—the linear blend of the matrices averages the values directly across the center.

This causes the intermediate transformation matrix to experience non-uniform scaling toward zero. Geometrically, the vertices collapse inward toward the central axis of the bone, creating a pinched, twisted effect universally known as the "candy-wrapper" artifact.

How Dual Quaternions Work

A dual quaternion extends classical quaternions using Clifford algebra and dual numbers. A dual number has the form \(a + \epsilon b\), where \(\epsilon^2 = 0\) (with \(\epsilon \neq 0\)).

A dual quaternion combines two standard quaternions into an eight-component mathematical object:

\[\hat{q} = q_r + \epsilon q_d\]

  • Real part (\(q_r\)): A unit quaternion representing the rotational component of the transform.
  • Dual part (\(q_d\)): A quaternion representing the translation, calculated as \(q_d = \frac{1}{2} t q_r\), where \(t\) is the translation vector expressed as a pure quaternion.

Because a unit dual quaternion encodes a rigid transformation (rotation and translation) without permitting shearing or non-uniform scaling, it treats transformation space as a manifold rather than a Euclidean space.

Preventing Mesh Collapse During Severe Twists

Dual-quaternion skinning prevents mesh collapse by altering how the skinning weights are interpolated. Instead of interpolating matrices, the engine computes a Dual Quaternion Linear Blend (DLB):

\[\hat{b} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i \hat{q}_i}{\left\| \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i \hat{q}_i \right\|}\]

Before blending, the dot product of the real parts of the quaternions is checked to ensure they lie on the same hemisphere (\(q_{r,i} \cdot q_{r,0} \ge 0\)), preventing antipodal blending issues. Once normalized, the resulting dual quaternion represents a pure rigid transformation.

When a joint undergoes a severe twist:

  1. Curvilinear Interpolation: The rotation interpolates along a spherical arc rather than cutting through the center of the joint volume.
  2. Volume Preservation: Because the transformation remains rigid during interpolation, the vertices maintain their radial distance from the joint's axis of rotation.
  3. Pinch Elimination: Rather than collapsing inward toward the bone, the vertices rotate smoothly around the bone axis, effectively resolving the candy-wrapper artifact without manual corrective blend shapes.

Practical Considerations in Game Engines

While DQS resolves volume loss during severe joint twists, it introduces specific technical trade-offs that developers account for during implementation:

  • The "Joint Bulging" Effect: Because dual quaternions strictly preserve distance and volume, bending joints (such as knees or elbows) can sometimes exhibit an unnatural outward bulging artifact. Many modern game engines resolve this using Hybrid Skinning, which dynamically blends between LBS for bending joints and DQS for twisting joints based on vertex weights or bone type.
  • Lack of Scale Support: Standard unit dual quaternions only support rigid transformations (isometries). Scaling—especially non-uniform scaling—cannot be represented directly within a unit dual quaternion. Engines handling scalable rigs often separate scale into a secondary pass or apply it via a hybrid pipeline.
  • GPU Performance: Evaluating dual quaternions requires normalization on the GPU vertex shader. While slightly more instruction-heavy than a standard matrix-vector multiply, the 8-component structure requires only two float4 registers per bone instead of the three or four float4 registers needed for transformation matrices, reducing memory bandwidth pressure when passing bone palettes to shaders.
