How Game Studios Handle IP Clearance for Licensed Franchises

Developing a video game using a licensed intellectual property (IP)—such as a movie, comic book, or sports league—requires a rigorous legal and creative approval process. This article details how game studios navigate the complex landscape of IP clearance, from negotiating the initial licensing agreement and adhering to strict brand guidelines to managing multi-stage approval pipelines and planning for the eventual expiration of licensing rights.

Securing the Licensing Agreement

The IP clearance process begins long before development starts. Game studios must negotiate a licensing agreement with the IP holder (licensor). This contract defines the boundaries of how the IP can be used. Key components of these agreements include:

  • Scope of Rights: Specifies which characters, stories, and settings the studio can use.
  • Platform and Territory: Defines where the game can be sold (e.g., globally or in specific regions) and on which platforms (PC, console, mobile).
  • Financial Terms: Establishes royalty rates, advances, and minimum guarantees that the studio must pay the licensor.
  • Term Duration: Sets the timeframe for how long the studio has the right to develop and sell the game.

Adhering to Brand Guidelines

Once the contract is signed, the licensor provides the game studio with a comprehensive style guide. This document outlines the strict rules regarding the franchise’s visual identity, character behaviors, lore, and tone. For example, a style guide might dictate the exact color codes for a character's costume or forbid a heroic character from using lethal weapons. Staying within these guardrails is essential to prevent costly redesigns later in development.

The Multi-Stage Approval Pipeline

To ensure compliance with the brand guidelines, studios and licensors establish a structured approval pipeline. Developers must submit their work at various milestones throughout the production cycle:

  1. Concept Phase: Concept art, narrative outlines, and script drafts are submitted to ensure the story fits the franchise's established canon.
  2. Production Phase: 3D models, animations, voice-over recordings, and level designs are reviewed for visual and auditory accuracy.
  3. Pre-Release Phase: Fully playable builds are tested by the licensor to ensure the gameplay experience aligns with the brand's quality standards.

Delays in this approval pipeline can derail a project's timeline, making constant communication between the studio's licensing managers and the licensor's brand team critical.

Managing Third-Party IP Clearance

Working with a licensed franchise often introduces secondary IP clearance challenges. Even if a studio has the rights to a movie franchise, they may not automatically own the rights to the actors' likenesses, specific musical scores, or real-world car brands featured in that movie. Studios must identify these elements and secure separate talent agreements or third-party licenses to avoid copyright infringement.

Post-Launch Compliance and License Expiration

IP clearance responsibilities continue after the game launches. Live-service updates, downloadable content (DLC), and marketing materials all require ongoing approval from the licensor.

Furthermore, game studios must plan for the end of the license term. When a licensing agreement expires, the studio usually loses the right to sell the game. Studios handle this by building "sell-off" periods into their contracts, allowing them to clear remaining physical inventory, or by preparing to delist the game from digital storefronts while maintaining multiplayer servers for existing players.
