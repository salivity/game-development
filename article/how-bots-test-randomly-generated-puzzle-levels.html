How Bots Test Randomly Generated Puzzle Levels

Automated testing bots are essential tools in procedural puzzle game development, allowing studios to validate millions of randomly generated levels in minutes. By simulating player actions through search algorithms, heuristic models, and machine learning, these bots verify that procedurally generated puzzles are functionally solvable, mathematically fair, and aligned with intended difficulty curves before reaching human players.

Solvability Verification Through Graph Search

At the core of solvability testing is state-space search. A puzzle level can be represented as a directed graph where nodes represent game states (board configurations) and edges represent valid player moves. Automated bots use classic search algorithms such as Breadth-First Search (BFS), Depth-First Search (DFS), or A* to explore this graph.

The bot initializes at the starting configuration and systematically executes valid actions until it either encounters the winning state or exhausts all possibilities. If the algorithm cannot find a path to the goal, the level generation seed is flagged as unsolvable and discarded. For complex puzzles with large state spaces, developers often use Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) to navigate deep decision trees without needing to evaluate every single permutation.

Measuring Balance and Difficulty

Once solvability is confirmed, bots evaluate balance by measuring the physical and cognitive effort required to win. Key metrics collected during these automated runs include:

  • Minimum Moves to Win: Determined by optimal solvers (like A* with an admissible heuristic), setting the baseline for the puzzle's minimum complexity.
  • Branching Factor: The number of valid moves available at each turn. A high branching factor increases cognitive load, whereas a low branching factor may make a level feel linear or trivial.
  • Choke Points and Critical Moves: Situations where only a single specific move prevents a loss. Bots detect how unforgiving a level is by counting how many paths lead to irreversible failure.

Simulating Varied Player Personas

A level balanced purely for an optimal AI solver will often feel frustrating to a human. To capture realistic human behavior, developers deploy tiers of bots with varying artificial intelligence profiles:

  1. Optimal Bots: Always make the mathematically best move with full lookahead, representing top-tier or guide-following players.
  2. Heuristic/Casual Bots: Prioritize immediate short-term rewards (such as clearing the largest match in a match-3 game) with limited lookahead, mimicking typical casual play.
  3. Random/Noise Bots: Make random valid moves to test worst-case resilience and identify accidental softlocks.

By comparing the win rates and move counts across these different bot tiers, developers can assign precise difficulty ratings. For example, a level that an optimal bot wins 100% of the time, but a casual bot wins only 40% of the time, is calibrated as a "Hard" level suitable for later stages of the game.

Detecting Softlocks and Dead Ends

In games featuring physics, pushable blocks, or limited resources (such as Sokoban variants), levels can easily enter unwinnable states known as softlocks. Bots detect these conditions by tracking state reversibility. If an action permanently reduces the reachable state space and isolates the goal state from the current node, the bot flags the situation as a terminal dead end. Developers can then adjust the generation constraints to prevent generating layouts prone to early, unavoidable dead ends.

The Generate-and-Test Pipeline

In modern development workflows, automated testing bots run directly inside the procedural generation pipeline. When the generator outputs a level candidate, the headless game client passes the layout to the bot framework. The bot executes hundreds of simulations across various personas. If the candidate achieves the target win-rate threshold, move-count distribution, and absence of softlocks, the seed is approved and added to the game's level pool. If it fails, the seed is rejected or mutated in real time.
