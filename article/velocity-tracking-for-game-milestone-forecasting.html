Velocity Tracking for Game Milestone Forecasting

In iterative game development, producers face the challenge of reconciling dynamic creative iteration with fixed milestone deadlines. This article outlines how producers effectively measure team velocity, account for creative churn and production volatility, and apply probabilistic forecasting to predict milestone completion dates with high confidence.

Standardize the Unit of Estimation

Before velocity can be measured, teams must adopt a shared, stable estimation standard. Producers typically implement story points or ideal engineering days to estimate backlog items. To keep estimations accurate across disciplines:

  • Define Cross-Discipline Baselines: Establish reference tasks for disciplines such as gameplay programming, 3D art, and level design to align on what constitutes a 1-, 3-, or 5-point effort.
  • Agree on a Definition of Done (DoD): Ensure a feature is only counted as complete once it is integrated into the build, playtested, and verified by QA. Incomplete work contributes zero points to the velocity.

Measure Rolling Velocity Over Fixed Iterations

Velocity represents the number of points completed within a single sprint (typically two weeks). Because game production fluctuates due to technical hurdles and design shifts, relying on a single sprint’s velocity leads to inaccurate projections.

Producers track a rolling average of the past three to five sprints. This rolling average smooths out temporary spikes caused by crunch or dips caused by studio holidays, engine upgrades, or unexpected bug-fixing periods.

Map the Milestone Backlog

A milestone rarely consists of a completely refined backlog; early features are well-defined, while later deliverables remain abstract. To prepare for forecasting:

  1. Groom the Milestone Scope: Break down core deliverables into estimated tasks.
  2. Apply T-Shirt Sizing to Ambiguous Items: Assign coarse estimates (e.g., Small = 3 pts, Medium = 8 pts, Large = 20 pts) to high-level epics that lack technical breakdowns.
  3. Account for Undiscovered Work: Add an empirical contingency factor (often 20% to 35% in game development) to the total milestone backlog to account for creative discovery and design refactoring.

Use Probabilistic Forecasting Instead of Single-Point Dates

Dividing total backlog points by average velocity yields a single, deterministic date that rarely accounts for real-world risks. Skilled producers forecast using ranges based on standard deviation or Monte Carlo simulations:

  • Optimistic Forecast: Backlog size divided by the team’s highest sustained rolling velocity (e.g., 90th percentile).
  • Pessimistic Forecast: Total backlog plus contingency, divided by the team's lowest recorded velocity (e.g., 10th percentile).
  • Realistic (P50) Forecast: Average backlog divided by average rolling velocity.

This creates a delivery window (e.g., "The Alpha milestone will land between October 12 and November 2 with 85% probability"), giving stakeholders actionable confidence intervals rather than arbitrary deadlines.

Factor in Non-Linear Game Development Phases

Velocity tracking often assumes a constant linear output, which does not reflect the game production lifecycle. Producers adjust velocity forecasts based on the specific phase of development:

  • Pre-Production: Velocity is volatile and generally lower due to rapid prototyping, technical evaluation, and discarded work.
  • Production: Velocity stabilizes as tools, pipelines, and workflows mature.
  • Hardening and Polish: As milestones approach (such as Vertical Slice, Alpha, or Beta), feature velocity drops significantly as focus shifts to bug fixing, optimization, and platform certification requirements. Producers must reduce projected feature velocity to account for dedicated hardening sprints.

Monitor Trends with Burn-Up Charts

Producers track progress using milestone burn-up charts that display two separate lines: total scope and total completed work over time.

If the scope line rises faster than the completion line, the forecast automatically extends. This visual data enables producers to intervene early—either by cutting non-essential scope, adjusting the milestone delivery date, or shifting development capacity—long before the deadline is compromised.
