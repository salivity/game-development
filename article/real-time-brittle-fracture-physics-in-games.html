Real-Time Brittle Fracture Physics in Games

Computing real-time stress fractures on brittle crystalline structures requires game developers to simulate physical stress propagation, identify anisotropic cleavage planes, and dynamically split 3D meshes without dropping frame rates. By combining continuum mechanics approximations, directional Voronoi tessellation, and GPU-driven mesh slicing, engines can convincingly depict brittle crystal impacts at interactive speeds. This article details the mathematical models, geometric algorithms, and optimization pipelines used to achieve this effect.

1. Modeling Impact Force and Stress Propagation

When a projectile or collision strikes a crystalline surface, the game engine registers an impact impulse characterized by contact point, contact normal, and kinetic energy. In realistic offline simulations, this triggers a full Finite Element Method (FEM) analysis. In real-time game development, engines approximate FEM using dynamic impulse propagation or simplified mass-spring networks.

Developers convert the initial collision force into a localized stress tensor. Because brittle structures exhibit almost no plastic deformation, the engine relies on the Rankine maximum principal stress criterion: if the local tensile stress exceeds the material's critical tensile threshold, fracture initiation begins instantaneously without prior bending or stretching.

2. Crystallographic Cleavage vs. Isotropic Fracture

Unlike amorphous glass or uniform concrete, crystals possess an internal periodic atomic lattice. Fractures do not spread randomly; they follow predetermined crystallographic orientations known as cleavage planes (e.g., cubic, octahedral, or rhombohedral cleavage).

To model this, developers define a set of normal vectors \(\vec{N}_c\) representing the crystal’s dominant cleavage planes within the object’s local transform space. When the resolved stress tensor projects onto these planes and exceeds the material’s directional yield strength, crack propagation is constrained along these precise angles. This constraint generates the sharp, flat, reflective facets typical of broken quartz, calcite, or gemstones.

3. Procedural Geometry Generation

Once the engine calculates where and along which angles fractures occur, it modifies the geometry. Developers generally use two approaches:

  • Directional Voronoi Tessellation: Standard Voronoi fracture divides a mesh using randomly distributed seed points. For crystals, developers bias the seed distribution along stress wave trajectories and align cell boundaries with the active cleavage planes. Centroids cluster densely at the impact epicenter and disperse outward, generating sharp, angular shards.
  • Dynamic Mesh Slicing: For fully runtime, non-precalculated fractures, engines use plane-mesh clipping algorithms. The engine defines multiple cutting planes oriented along the active cleavage vectors passing through the impact point. Triangles intersecting the clipping planes are split, new vertices are interpolated, and cross-sectional caps are generated to seal the newly exposed interior faces.

4. GPU Acceleration via Compute Shaders

Real-time slicing is computationally expensive on the CPU. Modern engines offload geometric fracturing to the GPU using compute shaders:

  1. Vertex Evaluation: The compute shader reads the object’s vertex and index buffers alongside impact data.
  2. Plane Intersection Tests: Vertices are classified as positive, negative, or intersecting the cleavage planes.
  3. Topology Generation: The shader outputs new triangle indices directly into GPU memory, skipping CPU-to-GPU data transfer overhead.
  4. UV Mapping and Normals: Newly created cap geometry automatically receives planar UV coordinates and sharp vertex normals, ensuring fractured facets reflect light cleanly like freshly broken crystal.

5. Rigid Body Instantiation and Physics Clustering

Immediately after geometry generation, the severed mesh pieces are decoupled into individual rigid bodies. Each shard receives a calculated mass, center of gravity, and an initial velocity vector derived from the original impact energy and the shockwave’s radial expansion.

To prevent physics engines from choking on hundreds of interacting colliders, developers group smaller fragments into non-simulated particle systems or debris clusters. Micro-shards and crystalline dust are handled entirely by GPU particle emitters, while only the primary structural pieces receive full rigid-body collision shapes.
