Third-Person Camera Metrics for Level Design

Designing environments for third-person games requires scaling architecture beyond real-world dimensions to prevent camera collision, abrupt zooming, and visual clipping. Because third-person cameras rely on an offset boom (or spring arm) trailing the character, spatial bounds must accommodate both the avatar's collision capsule and the camera’s orbit radius. This guide outlines the essential spatial metrics for doorways, stairs, and ceilings to ensure smooth camera clearance and player comfort.

The Foundation: The Camera Rig Envelope

Before setting architectural metrics, you must define your game's camera envelope. A standard third-person rig consists of:

  • Player Capsule: Typically 1.8 meters high by 0.6 to 0.8 meters wide.
  • Camera Arm (Boom): Usually extends 2.5 to 4 meters behind the player, offset vertically by 1.2 to 1.8 meters.
  • Collision Probe (Spring Arm Sphere): A collision radius (typically 0.15 to 0.3 meters) around the camera that detects geometry and pushes the camera forward to prevent clipping through walls.
  • Field of View (FOV): Usually between 70 and 90 degrees.

Environmental dimensions are governed not by the player's physical size, but by the outer bounds of this camera envelope.

Doorway Dimensions

Real-world doors are approximately 0.8 to 0.9 meters wide and 2.0 to 2.1 meters high. In a third-person game, these dimensions cause the spring arm to collide with the lintel or frame, rapidly snapping the camera into the back of the player’s head.

  • Minimum Width: 1.5 to 2.0 meters. This allows the player to enter at an angle without the camera striking the doorframe and stuttering forward.
  • Combat/Action Width: 2.5 to 3.5 meters. High-speed movement, strafing, or flanking during combat requires wide entryways so the camera maintains its offset during directional shifts.
  • Minimum Height: 2.8 to 3.2 meters. A doorway needs sufficient clearance above the character’s head so that an elevated camera pitching downward does not strike the door frame.
  • Transitional Thickness: Limit the depth of the doorway or threshold to under 0.5 meters. Deep, narrow archways trap the camera in an extended zoom-in state.

Ceiling and Overhead Clearance

Low ceilings are the primary cause of disorienting camera snaps. When a camera's collision sphere hits an overhead surface, the spring arm collapses downward and forward.

  • Standard Interior Clearance: 3.5 to 4.5 meters. This accommodates a character height of 1.8 meters, an elevated camera angle (15–20 degrees downward pitch), and a 3-meter boom without geometric contact.
  • Tight/Claustrophobic Spaces: 3.0 meters. This is the functional minimum for a standard over-the-shoulder perspective. Anything lower forces the camera directly onto the character's shoulder or into a forced first-person perspective.
  • Combat and Dynamic Camera Interiors: 5.0 to 6.0+ meters. Games featuring jumping, vertical combat, or dynamic boss encounters require taller volumes to prevent the camera from clipping ceiling geometry when pitching up to frame targets.

Staircases and Vertical Transitions

Stairs compound clearance issues because the ground behind the character rises as they descend, or the ceiling lowers relative to the camera as they ascend.

  • Headroom Clearance: 3.5 to 4.0 meters, measured vertically from the nose of each tread to the ceiling directly above it. Insufficient headroom causes the camera to ride the ceiling when moving up the stairs.
  • Staircase Width: 2.0 to 3.0 meters. Enclosed stairwells narrower than 2 meters push the camera into the wall during turns, especially on spiral or switchback stairs.
  • Incline Angle: Keep the slope between 25 and 33 degrees (an approximate 2:1 run-to-rise ratio, such as 30 cm tread to 15 cm riser). Steeper angles force the ground behind the player upward too quickly, instantly colliding with a trailing camera during ascents.
  • Landing Depths: Landings should be at least 2.5 to 3.0 meters deep. This gives the spring arm enough time to smoothly interpolate its vertical position before the player changes elevation or turns 90 to 180 degrees.

Camera Collision Geometry Optimization

Metrics alone do not solve all camera issues; collision design is equally critical:

  • Invisible Camera Ramps: Place smooth, invisible collision volumes over stairs. The player's physics capsule walks the steps, but the camera collision traces against a smooth 30-degree incline, eliminating jitter.
  • Beveled Corners: Use angled collision volumes on sharp interior corners and doorway frames. This prevents the camera sphere from catching on 90-degree edges.
  • Camera-Only Collision Channels: Separate player collision from camera collision. Thin decorative props, light fixtures, and low arches should block the player or be visually present, but set to ignore camera traces to prevent unnecessary view snapping.
