Dynamic Mud and Snow Displacement Shaders in Games

Technical artists create dynamic, interactive mud and snow displacement on terrain by combining top-down render target capture systems with vertex displacement and dynamic surface shading. This process tracks moving actors to record deformation masks, offsets the terrain geometry in real time, and updates material properties—such as roughness, normal maps, and albedo—to reveal underlying surfaces and simulate realistic physical deformation.

1. Trail Capture via Render Targets

The deformation pipeline begins by recording character and vehicle interactions onto a dynamic texture known as a Render Target.

  • Orthographic Projection: An orthographic scene capture camera is positioned above the player, pointing downward, and locked to follow the player’s coordinates within a discrete grid.
  • Mask Generation: Colliders, skeletal meshes, or specialized visual proxy meshes attached to feet and tires are rendered onto this canvas. Areas where meshes intersect the ground write a depth or mask value to specific texture channels.
  • Persistent Buffering and Dissipation: To support persistent trails, developers use ping-pong render targets (swapping between two textures). A compute shader or custom blend material gradually clears or blends the mask over time to simulate melting snow or mud settling.

2. Geometry Deformation Techniques

Once the interaction mask is stored in the render target, it is passed to the terrain shader to deform the actual geometry.

  • Vertex Displacement: In the vertex shader stage, the sampled interaction texture acts as a negative heightmap. Vertices located within the masked areas are pushed downward along their vertex normals or the world Z-axis.
  • Tessellation and Dynamic Subdivisions: Traditional workflows rely on distance-based hardware tessellation to dynamically add geometry near the camera where deformation occurs.
  • Modern Terrain Meshes: Modern engines often utilize systems like Unreal Engine’s Virtual Heightfield Mesh or custom GPU-driven compute grids. These systems provide dense, optimized geometry strictly within the camera view, preventing the high performance costs of full-terrain subdivision.

3. Procedural Normal and Material Updates

Pushover geometry creates stretched textures and incorrect lighting if surface attributes are not updated alongside the height changes.

  • Normal Reconstruction: Because the original mesh normals do not reflect the newly displaced shape, real-time normal calculation is required. Shaders use Sobel filtering or cross-product sampling of adjacent pixels in the heightmap to generate updated tangent space or world space normals.
  • Layer Blending: The mask controls a linear interpolation (lerp) between different surface layers. In snow shaders, displacement reveals a compacted ice or dirt layer below. In mud shaders, displacement exposes high-moisture, darker albedo maps.
  • Roughness and Wetness Modulation: Mud tracks typically decrease surface roughness and increase specular highlights to simulate standing water in the grooves. Snow tracks reduce subsurface scattering and sparkle intensity in compressed areas to mimic high-density packed snow.

4. Performance Optimization

Dynamic displacement can be GPU-intensive, requiring strict optimization strategies:

  • Resolution and Bounds Clamping: Render targets are constrained to small, localized areas (e.g., 2048x2048 covering a 30x30 meter box around the player) rather than covering the entire map.
  • Distance Falloff: Displacement logic and high-frequency normal reconstruction fade out over short distances, transitioning back to static terrain rendering.
  • Channel Packing: Multiple interaction layers—such as depth displacement, edge buildup (rims around footprints), and surface wetness—are packed into separate channels (RGBA) of a single texture to minimize texture memory overhead.
