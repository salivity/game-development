Destructive QA Testing for Game Physics Systems

This article outlines how Quality Assurance (QA) leads design and execute destructive exploratory testing sessions to expose vulnerabilities in newly introduced video game physics engines. From scoping charters and isolating high-risk integration vectors—such as collision detection, mass extremes, and frame-rate discrepancies—to leveraging debug tools and structured swarming, this guide details practical strategies to identify, reproduce, and document severe physics anomalies before production code stabilizes.

Establish Targeted Test Charters

Unstructured exploratory testing often yields redundant or unhelpful feedback. QA leads prevent this by using Session-Based Test Management (SBTM) to define focused charters. Rather than issuing a generic mandate to "break physics," leads assign specific mechanical vectors to individual testers or pairs.

Common targeted charters include:

  • Volumetric Bounds and Tunneling: Attempting to force high-velocity rigidbodies through thin static colliders (Continuous Collision Detection validation).
  • Mass and Scale Extremes: Testing how the solver handles interactions between objects with extreme mass ratios (e.g., a 10,000 kg actor colliding with a 0.01 kg prop).
  • Constraint and Joint Stability: Straining hinges, springs, and ragdoll joints to identify explosive physics loops or detached geometry.
  • State Transitions: Moving rapidly between physics states, such as ragdoll-to-animation recovery, swimming-to-falling, or mounting vehicles.

Implement Swarming and Timeboxing

Destructive sessions are most effective when executed as timeboxed "bug bashes" or swarms lasting between 60 to 90 minutes. Extending beyond this window leads to tester fatigue and diminished creativity.

QA leads typically divide the team into pairs consisting of a driver (interacting with the game) and a navigator (logging logs, noting environmental variables, and checking system telemetry). Pairing increases the likelihood of identifying non-deterministic bugs, as the navigator observes systemic patterns that the driver might miss during active play.

Attack Critical Physics Vulnerabilities

Physics engines rely on approximations and mathematical trade-offs to maintain real-time performance. Leads direct testers to exploit the typical weak points of these approximations:

1. Frame Rate Decoupling and Fluctuations

Physics engines often run on fixed timesteps, while rendering operates on variable frame rates. Leads instruct testers to artificially alter performance metrics:

  • Artificially cap the client at 10–15 FPS while spawning high quantities of dynamic objects to test accumulator behavior.
  • Unlock frame rates to extreme highs (144+ FPS) to check for floating-point calculation acceleration or physics simulation drift.
  • Introduce sudden lag spikes (via network throttling or memory injection) precisely as high-impact collisions occur.

2. Geometry Cramming and Overlapping Colliders

Testers place dynamic props inside enclosed spaces, such as elevators, closing doors, or corners between static meshes. By wedging objects together, testers force collision solvers into impossible resolve loops, resulting in catastrophic velocity generation, commonly referred to as "object launching."

3. Infinite Stacking and Micro-Vibrations

Testers create vertical stacks of dynamic items to evaluate how resting contact is resolved. Stable systems quickly transition resting bodies into a "sleep" state to save compute cycles. Flawed implementations fail to sleep, producing micro-vibrations that propagate through the stack until the simulation explodes.

Equip Testers with Debug and Telemetry Tooling

Destructive testing relies on specialized internal tooling to manipulate the simulation environment. Leads ensure testers have access to:

  • Physics Visualization Overlays: Real-time rendering of bounding boxes (AABBs), collision meshes, contact points, and normal vectors to verify whether visual meshes align with underlying physics assets.
  • Object Spawners and Velocity Injectors: Developer commands to spawn predefined batches of physics actors at set velocities and trajectories.
  • Time-Dilation Controls: The ability to pause, advance tick-by-tick, or slow time to isolate the exact moment of solver failure.
  • State Snapshots: Quick-save mechanisms that capture object transforms, momentum vectors, and internal states right before an interaction occurs, allowing rapid reproduction of non-deterministic anomalies.

Standardize Documentation for Non-Deterministic Bugs

Physics defects are notoriously difficult to reproduce due to millisecond timing differences and floating-point variations. QA leads mandate strict logging protocols during destructive sessions:

  • Deterministic Capture: Testers must run continuous background video recording with debug overlays visible (showing coordinates, current frame rate, and input history).
  • Physics State Dumps: Bug reports must include raw positional data, angular velocity, linear velocity, and active constraints at the moment of failure.
  • Seed Logging: If the engine relies on pseudo-random seeds for procedural impacts or ragdoll twitching, the session seed must be automatically logged directly into the defect tracker.
