How to Build VR Climbing Mechanics with Motion Controllers

Building virtual reality climbing mechanics requires translating independent hand-tracking inputs into fluid, full-body locomotion while maintaining physical collision and spatial stability. This article breaks down the foundational architecture behind VR climbing systems, covering collision-based grip detection, inverse displacement movement mathematics, dual-hand arbitration, physics resolution to prevent clipping, and momentum transfer for leaping mechanics.

Grip Detection and Surface Interaction

The climbing loop begins with detecting interaction between the player's hand and the climbing surface. Developers implement this using trigger-based collider volumes—typically spheres or capsules attached to the hand bones of each motion controller. When a player brings their virtual hand into proximity with an object designated as climbable (often defined via dedicated physics layers or gameplay tags) and presses the grip input, the system establishes a grip state.

To support realistic ledge grabbing versus free-surface climbing, developers use one of two approaches:

  • Geometry-agnostic climbing: Any surface tagged Climbable can be grabbed anywhere.
  • Snap-point climbing: Gripping queries the nearest point on a spline or predefined ledge volume, locking the virtual hand's visual transform to the ledge while tracking the physical controller's offset.

Inverse Displacement Locomotion

Once a hand establishes a grip, the standard locomotion logic (such as thumbstick movement or gravity) is temporarily disabled or overridden. VR climbing uses an inverse displacement model: instead of moving the hands relative to the player, the player rig moves relative to the anchored hand.

  1. Anchor Point Capture: At the moment of the grip event, the system records the world-space position of the gripping controller as an anchor point.
  2. Delta Calculation: On each subsequent physics frame (FixedUpdate), the engine measures the controller's current local position relative to its position in the previous frame: \[\Delta \vec{P} = \vec{P}_{\text{current}} - \vec{P}_{\text{previous}}\]
  3. Player Rig Translation: The negative of this delta is applied to the root VR origin or character controller: \[\vec{P}_{\text{rig}} = \vec{P}_{\text{rig}} - \Delta \vec{P}\]

This makes the virtual world feel anchored to the player’s hand. Pulling the controller downward moves the player character upward by the exact same distance.

Dual-Hand Arbitration and Rotation

When both hands grip surfaces simultaneously, the system must resolve conflicting displacement vectors. Developers typically handle this through one of three methods:

  • Dominant Hand (Last Grabbed): The most recent hand to grip takes complete control of translation, while the secondary hand remains visually clamped to its hold.
  • Midpoint Averaging: The system calculates the displacement delta for both hands and applies the average vector to the player rig.
  • Two-Handed Rigid Transform: By tracking both controllers, developers compute both a translation delta and an angular rotation delta between the two hand positions. This allows players to physically pivot, tilt, and twist their virtual body along the axis defined between their two grip points.

Physics Resolution and Collision Avoidance

Directly translating the player's transform can push their virtual collision capsule through walls or terrain. To prevent clipping, developers apply the inverse displacement vector using physics-based movement functions (such as Unity’s CharacterController.Move or Unreal Engine’s MoveUpdatedComponent) rather than setting raw positions.

If a movement step causes the player's head or torso collider to intersect geometry, the collision engine stops forward progress along that normal while allowing sliding along unrestricted axes.

Velocity Transfer and Momentum Mechanics

To make climbing dynamic, systems record the velocity of the gripping hand over a short rolling window (typically the last 3 to 5 frames). When the player releases the grip button while rapidly moving their arm, the system:

  1. Averages the recorded hand movement vectors to determine release velocity.
  2. Re-enables gravity and physics simulation on the player's character body.
  3. Injects the inverted release velocity as an impulse vector into the player's physics component, launching the player upward or outward.

Providing a short burst of haptic vibration at the moment of grip confirmation and momentum release ensures the physical controllers deliver the tactile feedback necessary for intuitive climbing.
