Creating Procedural Bone Crunches in Horror Games

This article explores how audio engineers design procedural bone-breaking audio by combining synthetic audio textures, physical modeling, and runtime middleware logic. By moving away from static, repetitive audio samples, horror game sound designers utilize modular synthesis layers—spanning sub-bass impacts, transient snaps, and granular debris—to simulate dynamic, non-repeating skeletal fractures tailored to in-game physics.

Anatomy of a Procedural Bone Impact

A visceral bone crunch consists of distinct frequency bands and temporal stages that must be synthesized and dynamically aligned:

  • The Sub/Transient Impact (20 Hz – 150 Hz): The initial blunt-force trauma representing mass and kinetic transfer.
  • The Structural Fracture (200 Hz – 2 kHz): The dense, woody resistance and failure of dense calcium and cartilage.
  • The Brittle Crackle (2 kHz – 12 kHz): The high-frequency snap of splintering fragments, accompanied by wet, tearing tissue textures.

Generating Synthetic Base Textures

Rather than relying purely on traditional organic foley, procedural systems use sound synthesis to generate infinitely variable source material:

  1. Modal Synthesis for Rigid Bodies: Resonant filter banks driven by impulse exciters simulate the physical properties of rigid, hollow, or marrow-filled bone structures. By automating the damping and decay parameters, the audio engine alters perceived bone density and thickness.
  2. Frequency Modulation (FM) for the Fracture Snap: 2-operator or 4-operator FM synthesis patches with rapid envelope decays generate sharp, metallic-to-woody transient spikes. Modulating the carrier-to-modulator ratio introduces dissonance, making the snap sound unnatural and structurally violent.
  3. Noise-Based Friction and Tearing: Filtered white and pink noise, shaped with stochastic amplitude envelopes and dynamic comb filtering, simulates the friction of jagged fragments grinding against one another and tearing adjacent muscle fibers.

Granular Processing of Splinter Textures

To give synthesized tones an organic, unpredictable quality, engineers feed synthesized micro-transients into real-time granular synthesis engines. The system divides source material into microscopic grains (between 10 ms and 80 ms) and scatters them across time, pitch, and stereo positioning.

By tying grain density and random pitch modulation to impact forces, the granular engine naturally generates irregular splintering sequences, preventing the "machine-gun effect" common in traditional one-shot sample playback.

Dynamic Runtime Parameter Mapping

In game audio middleware such as Wwise, FMOD, or Unreal Engine’s MetaSounds, the synthesized components are assigned to real-time parameters derived from physics data:

  • Impact Velocity: Scaled directly to the low-end transient decay, the FM modulator index, and master distortion to reflect the severity of the trauma.
  • Damage Vector and Mass: Controls the resonance frequency of the modal synthesis layer. A heavy blunt weapon striking a skull outputs lower, denser resonance than a snapping finger joint.
  • Structural Rupture States: Multi-stage breaks use state logic to sequence events: pre-impact tension, the primary snap, secondary splintering, and settling debris.

Transient Shaping and Distortion Layering

Raw synthesized textures lack the visceral weight required for modern horror titles without targeted post-processing. Procedural signal chains route synthesized layers through multi-band saturation and transient designers.

Saturating mid-band frequencies introduces harmonic overtones that mimic acoustic distortion inside an ear canal during extreme trauma, while aggressive dynamic look-ahead limiting forces the transient crackle above the rest of the soundscape, maximizing psychological shock.
