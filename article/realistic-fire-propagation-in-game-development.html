Realistic Fire Propagation in Game Development

Simulating dynamic fire propagation across game environments requires a combination of mathematical simulation models, material data structures, and visual rendering techniques. Game developers achieve realistic fire behavior by calculating heat transfer, fuel consumption, and environmental influences such as wind and moisture. This article explores the primary technical approaches used in modern game engines to create dynamic, believable, and performant fire propagation systems.

1. Cellular Automata and 2D Texture Diffusion

One of the most common techniques, popularized by games like the Far Cry series, relies on 2D texture-space diffusion or cellular automata mapped across the terrain.

  • State Maps: The engine writes material properties (flammability, fuel amount, burn state, and temperature) into a 2D render target or texture mask applied to the environment.
  • Diffusion Compute Shaders: Every frame or tick, a compute shader calculates heat dispersion to adjacent pixels based on a heat transfer equation. When a cell's temperature surpasses the material's ignition threshold, its state shifts to burning, steadily depleting its fuel.
  • Visual Coupling: Shaders read these texture values to blend between unburned, charred, and ash material states, while driving procedural particle spawning for flames and smoke.

2. Node and Graph-Based Propagation

For environments composed of discrete 3D objects (such as wooden structures, foliage, and clutter), developers often use node or graph networks.

  • Proximity Graphs: Objects within a certain radius dynamically form logical connections. Each object acts as a node storing localized data: current heat, ignition point, burn duration, and structural integrity.
  • Raycast and Volume Checks: When a node ignites, it casts rays or expands spherical collision queries to detect nearby flammable objects. Heat is transferred proportionally based on distance and angle, avoiding the need for a global grid.
  • Hierarchical Destruction: Once an object’s fuel is fully consumed, it triggers structural events, such as collapsing physics constraints or swapping to a fractured mesh variant.

3. 3D Voxel Grids

For complex, multi-layered environments with verticality, developers implement sparse 3D voxel grids.

  • Volumetric Heat Transfer: Fire and hot air rise naturally. Voxel grids allow simulation of 3D convection currents, where heat cells transfer energy upward and outward through 3D space.
  • Sparse Data Structures: To maintain performance, developers utilize sparse voxel octrees or memory-efficient dynamic grids that only allocate voxel data in active burning regions.

4. Environmental and Physical Influences

Dynamic fire requires responsiveness to real-time physical forces in the game world:

  • Wind Vectors: Global or localized wind vectors bias heat transfer equations, pushing the propagation vector downwind and causing fire to spread faster in the direction of the airflow.
  • Moisture and Weather: Dynamic weather systems modify the ignition threshold and burn rates of materials. Rain increases the required heat energy for ignition and accelerates cooling, while dry conditions lower thresholds.
  • Slope and Elevation: Fire naturally spreads faster uphill due to pre-heating of elevated fuels by rising hot air. Propagation formulas include dot products between the terrain normal and the vertical up-vector to simulate this behavior.

5. Performance Optimization Strategies

Simulating thousands of dynamic heat calculations simultaneously can be computationally expensive. Developers employ several optimization techniques:

  • Asynchronous Compute and Tick Slicing: Fire propagation logic does not need to run at the full rendering frame rate (e.g., 60 or 120 FPS). Running simulation updates at 10–20 Hz on asynchronous threads significantly reduces CPU/GPU overhead.
  • Spatial Culling and LODs: Fire systems outside the player's view or immediate vicinity transition to simplified statistical models or freeze entirely until the player approaches.
  • Particle Instancing: Visual representations of fire reuse instanced particle systems and dynamic point lights with aggressive radius limits to prevent excessive GPU fill-rate costs.
