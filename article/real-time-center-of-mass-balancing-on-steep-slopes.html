Real-Time Center-of-Mass Balancing on Steep Slopes

When non-player and player characters traverse steep terrain, standard walking animations often look rigid or unnatural, causing feet to float or torsos to tilt impossibly. Game developers maintain physical realism through real-time center-of-mass (CoM) balance offsets, which dynamically shift a character's skeleton to simulate equilibrium, counter gravity, and prevent tipping. This article examines the core mathematical, kinematic, and physics-based methods used in modern game engines to calculate and apply these dynamic balance adjustments.

Surface Normal Sampling and Slope Projection

The baseline for determining balance offsets begins with environmental probing. The engine casts multiple raycasts or sphere-sweeps—typically under each foot, beneath the pelvis, and ahead of the character’s movement vector—to gather surface normal vectors.

By calculating the angle between the average ground normal and the global up-vector (gravity), the system derives the slope gradient. To determine the necessary balance offset, developers project the character's target CoM along this incline. If moving uphill, the CoM is shifted forward to simulate driving power and prevent backward tipping; when descending, the CoM moves backward to counteract forward-tumbling momentum.

Pelvic Translation via Inverse Kinematics (IK)

The root bone or pelvis dictates the majority of a character's mass. In standard Foot Placement IK systems, adjusting the feet alone to hit uneven ground often hyper-extends the uphill leg or compresses the downhill leg unnaturally.

To maintain balance, engines offset the pelvis vertically and horizontally:

  • Vertical Pelvis Drop: The pelvis is dropped toward the lower foot by a calculated offset, ensuring both legs remain within their natural kinematic reach without locking knees.
  • Lateral and Longitudinal Offsets: Using trigonometric functions based on the slope angle (\(\theta\)), the pelvis is translated horizontally along the surface plane. This maintains the CoM projection within the polygon of support formed by the contact points of the feet.

The Linear Inverted Pendulum Model (LIPM) and Zero Moment Point (ZMP)

Derived from bipedal robotics, the Linear Inverted Pendulum Model (LIPM) simplifies the character's complex mass into a single point mass balanced on a massless leg. Combined with Zero Moment Point (ZMP) calculations, this method determines where the net dynamic force acting on the character intersects the ground.

On a steep slope:

  1. The game evaluates inertial forces (mass multiplied by linear acceleration) alongside the gravitational vector.
  2. The ZMP is computed to find the point where horizontal reaction forces create zero net torque.
  3. If the calculated ZMP drifts outside the polygon of support created by the feet on the incline, an offset vector is calculated to pull the CoM back toward dynamic equilibrium, preventing the character from visually "falling."

Spine Layering and Dynamic Leaning

Translating the pelvis is often insufficient on extreme inclines; the upper body must actively lean. Developers use procedural spine adjustments layered on top of locomotion animations:

  • Pitch Offsets: Rotational offsets applied along the local pitch axis of the spine bones (lumbar and thoracic) bend the upper torso toward the slope when moving uphill and away from the slope when moving downhill.
  • Spring-Damper Systems: Instead of applying static angle offsets, developers route the calculated lean angle through second-order systems or Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) controllers. This introduces momentum, inertia, and slight overshooting, mimicking how natural muscles stabilize shifting weight.

Physical Animation and Rigidbody Simulation

For the highest fidelity, modern engines blend kinematic keyframe animations with real-time rigid body dynamics. The character’s ragdoll or physics asset is connected to the kinematic skeleton via physics constraints (angular and linear springs).

When walking on a steep slope, gravity naturally pulls the simulated physical body parts downward. The game calculates the divergence between the desired pose and the physically deflected pose, using the discrepancy as an automated balance offset. This results in realistic torso resistance, dynamic stumbling recovery, and an authentic shift of weight without requiring manually scripted kinematic offsets for every slope angle.
