Level Design Metrics: Calculating Movement and Jump Grids

Establishing player movement metrics and jump clearances is a foundational step in level design that bridges character mechanics with environment art. This article outlines the systematic process level designers use to extract kinematic data from character controllers, test mechanical limits in dedicated metric gyms, calculate vertical and horizontal jump arcs, and convert those measurements into a unified architectural grid that ensures intuitive gameplay and modular environment production.

Extracting Core Character Controller Variables

Before constructing an environment, designers must identify the exact variables governing the character controller. In modern engines like Unreal Engine or Unity, these variables dictate character physics:

  • Walk and Run Speed: Units per second (often centimeters per second in Unreal, or meters per second in Unity).
  • Initial Jump Velocity (\(V_0\)): The instantaneous upward impulse applied when pressing the jump button.
  • Gravity Scale (\(g\)): The downward acceleration pulling the character back to earth.
  • Air Control / Friction: The degree to which a player can modify their trajectory mid-flight.
  • Bounding Box Dimensions: The collision cylinder or capsule radius and height.

Calculating Jump Clearances

Jump clearance involves both vertical height (apex) and horizontal reach (distance traveled before landing). While physics engines handle real-time simulation, designers calculate theoretical maximums using standard kinematic formulas to determine baseline boundaries.

1. Vertical Clearance (Maximum Jump Height)

The peak of a standard jump occurs when vertical velocity reaches zero:

\[\text{Apex Height} = \frac{V_0^2}{2g}\]

If a character has an initial vertical velocity (\(V_0\)) of 600 cm/s and gravity (\(g\)) is 980 cm/s², the theoretical peak is:

\[\frac{600^2}{2 \times 980} \approx 183.6 \text{ cm}\]

Designers will typically round this to a practical limit (e.g., a maximum obstacle clearance of 175 cm to provide a margin for error).

2. Hang Time and Horizontal Distance

Hang time is the total duration the character spends in the air during a flat jump:

\[\text{Time to Apex } (t) = \frac{V_0}{g}\] \[\text{Total Hang Time} = 2t\]

Horizontal jump clearance is calculated by multiplying total hang time by forward movement speed (\(V_x\)):

\[\text{Max Distance} = V_x \times (2t)\]

If the player runs at 500 cm/s and hang time is 1.22 seconds, maximum horizontal clearance is approximately 610 cm.

Empirical Testing: The Metric Gym

Calculations provide theoretical maximums, but input latency, frame rates, and variable jump systems (holding the button for higher jumps) alter outcomes. Designers build a specialized level known as a metric gym or graybox test range to record practical gameplay tolerances.

In this gym, designers place standardized geometry at stepped intervals:

  • Vertical Ramps and Steps: Blocks placed in precise increments (e.g., 50 cm, 100 cm, 150 cm, 200 cm) to observe step-up limits, crouch heights, and vault trigger points.
  • Horizontal Gap Tests: Pits separated by increasing distances (e.g., 200 cm to 800 cm in 50 cm increments) to find minimum, comfortable, and maximum jump distances.
  • Headroom Clearances: Ceilings set at varying heights above jumps to prevent camera clipping and unintended trajectory cutoffs.

From these tests, designers establish three key values for every action:

  1. Comfortable: Requires minimal precision; ideal for critical path progression.
  2. Challenging: Requires precise timing or sprint momentum; used for optional rewards or platforming challenges.
  3. Impossible: Absolute physical ceiling; used to construct soft boundaries and prevent sequence breaking.

Establishing the Modular Architectural Grid

Once physical limits are finalized, they are translated into a standardized grid for grayboxing and 3D asset creation. This prevents visual dissonance where architectural assets (such as doors or stairs) look unnatural relative to character scale.

  • Snapping Units: Engines typically use base-2 grids (8, 16, 32, 64, 128 units) or decimal metric systems (0.25m, 0.5m, 1m). The chosen system must neatly divide the character’s comfortable jump and step heights.
  • Step Height: Maximum height the character can traverse without jumping (typically 25–30 cm).
  • Doorway and Corridor Dimensions: Character capsule dimensions dictate minimum path widths. A capsule width of 80 cm typically requires a minimum doorway width of 120–150 cm to account for third-person camera clearance and combat spacing.
  • Cover Heights: Half-cover is aligned to the player's crouch height; full cover is aligned to the player's standing height plus a buffer to obscure the player model completely.
  • Ledge Grab/Mantle Windows: If the game supports mantling, the mantle reach distance is added to the jump apex to establish the total vertical traversal metric.

By aligning controller physics with an architectural grid early in production, level designers ensure that geometry can be assembled rapidly with modular kits while maintaining consistent movement readability across the entire game.
