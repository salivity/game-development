Game Feature Greenlight Approval Criteria

Passing the formal greenlight phase requires rigorous validation before a game moves from pre-production into costly full-scale development. This article outlines the specific criteria production teams, lead designers, and executive stakeholders use to determine whether a core game feature should be approved, cut, or iterated upon, focusing on playability, technical viability, production cost, and market alignment.

1. Proof of Fun and Core Loop Validation

A core feature must be demonstrably engaging in a playable prototype. Stakeholders evaluate:

  • The "30-Second Loop": Does the moment-to-moment interaction feel responsive, rewarding, and mechanically sound without relying on final art or sound assets?
  • Objective Playtest Data: Internal and external playtest results must show positive engagement metrics, intuitive player comprehension, and a desire for repeated play sessions.
  • Clarity of Purpose: The feature must clearly serve the game's high-level vision rather than existing as feature bloat.

2. Technical Feasibility and Performance Scalability

Engineering leads must prove that the feature can function efficiently at scale across all target platforms:

  • Engine and Architecture Stability: The feature must not require unproven tech or engine modifications that introduce disproportionate risk.
  • Performance Budgets: Memory usage, rendering costs, networking overhead, and frame-rate impacts must fit strictly within established platform constraints.
  • Pipeline Integration: Tools, shaders, and workflow pipelines required to support the feature must already exist or be fully mapped out.

3. Production Scope and Budget Feasibility

Executive producers assess the financial and scheduling impact of building, polishing, and maintaining the feature:

  • Asset and Labor Requirements: A complete estimate of the engineering, art, animation, audio, and QA hours required to bring the feature to shipping quality.
  • Schedule Alignment: Delivery dates for the feature must fit within the broader production roadmap without risking crunch or launch delays.
  • Content Dependency: The feature’s reliance on additional content (e.g., specific enemy types, custom UI, unique environments) must not exceed production capacity.

4. Market Fit and Competitive Advantage

Product managers evaluate how the feature positions the game within its commercial landscape:

  • Unique Selling Proposition (USP): Does the feature differentiate the game from key competitors in a marketable way?
  • Audience Expectations: Does the feature cater directly to the desires and genre conventions expected by the target demographic?
  • Streaming and Shareability: For modern titles, stakeholders assess whether the mechanic generates emergent, visually distinct moments that drive organic visibility on platforms like Twitch or YouTube.

5. Systemic Synergy and Extensibility

No core feature operates in a vacuum. It must interact harmoniously with other game systems:

  • Metagame Integration: The feature must tie logically into progression, economy, and reward systems.
  • Accessibility and UX: The feature must be adaptable to standard control schemes and accessible to players with varying physical capabilities.
  • Live-Ops Viability: If applicable, the feature must be modular enough to support future updates, patches, balance changes, or seasonal events post-launch.

6. Risk Profile and Mitigation Plan

Every approved feature must come with an actionable contingency plan:

  • Identified Failure Points: Clear documentation on what could go wrong regarding design balance, technical debt, or player churn.
  • Downscoping Path: A predefined method to scale back, simplify, or isolate the feature without breaking the core game if production bottlenecks occur.
