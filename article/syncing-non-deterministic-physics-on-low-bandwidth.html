Syncing Non-Deterministic Physics on Low Bandwidth

Synchronizing non-deterministic physics simulations across a network is one of the most complex challenges in multiplayer game development. Because non-deterministic engines produce divergent physical outcomes from identical initial inputs, developers must constantly transmit authoritative state updates to keep clients aligned. Over low-bandwidth connections, this necessity clashes directly with strict data limits, resulting in state divergence, visual artifacts, latency amplification, and complex reconciliation issues.

Compounding State Divergence

Non-deterministic physics engines introduce minute variations due to differences in CPU architectures, floating-point math implementations, threading models, and execution order. In a localized simulation, these tiny variances compound rapidly—a phenomenon known as the butterfly effect. A rigid body colliding with a corner might deflect slightly left on the server and slightly right on the client. Over several simulation ticks, this small deviation transforms into completely different world states, requiring frequent corrections from the authoritative host.

Bandwidth Saturation from State Snapshots

Because clients cannot reliably predict future physics states on their own, the server must continuously send full state updates. A complete rigid-body snapshot typically includes:

  • 3D position vectors (X, Y, Z)
  • Orientations (Quaternions: X, Y, Z, W)
  • Linear velocity vectors
  • Angular velocity vectors

Multiplying these parameters across dozens or hundreds of active dynamic objects quickly exceeds the data throughput limits of low-bandwidth connections. Without sufficient bandwidth to broadcast updates at tick rate, the server must lower update frequencies, widening the divergence window between updates.

Packet Loss and Latency Amplification

Low-bandwidth environments often suffer from packet loss, high latency, and jitter. When client-side prediction is applied to non-deterministic systems, lost state packets force the client to extrapolate physical motion blind. By the time a corrective packet finally arrives, the client's local object may be far removed from the server's authoritative position, rendering standard dead-reckoning algorithms ineffective and inaccurate.

Visual Snapping and Rubber-Banding

When authoritative corrections arrive over a constrained link, the discrepancy between the client's local simulation and the server's true state is often substantial. Correcting this difference instantly causes visible "teleporting" or snapping. While interpolation and smoothing algorithms can blend these corrections over time, severe divergence under low update rates leads to noticeable rubber-banding, unnatural floatiness, or objects phasing through static geometry during interpolation.

Complex Multi-Body and Constraint Failures

Interacting systems—such as ragdolls, vehicle suspensions, rope physics, and stacked crates—rely on constraint solvers. Under low bandwidth, updating every individual joint or constraint solver state is impossible. If clients only receive partial updates or delayed constraint resolutions, joint limits break, stacks collapse prematurely, and ragdolls exhibit violent stretching or physics explosions due to conflicting local constraints and server corrections.

Common Engineering Solutions and Trade-Offs

To manage non-deterministic synchronization under tight bandwidth constraints, developers rely on several architectural compromises:

  • Quantization and Delta Compression: Truncating floating-point precision and only sending changes (deltas) relative to previous confirmed states.
  • Interest Management: Limiting physics synchronization strictly to objects within the player's immediate interaction radius or line of sight.
  • Cosmetic vs. Gameplay Separation: Restricting server-synchronized physics strictly to gameplay-critical objects, while running purely cosmetic physics (debris, secondary foliage, minor props) entirely client-side without any network sync.
  • Adaptive Send Rates: Dynamically scaling the update rate and precision of physics bodies based on an object's velocity, proximity to players, and current network congestion.
