Balancing FOV for Ultrawide PC Monitors

Balancing field-of-view (FOV) settings across ultrawide monitors is a critical technical requirement in modern PC game development. As display aspect ratios expand from standard 16:9 to 21:9 and super-ultrawide 32:9, developers must implement specific scaling algorithms, projection techniques, and camera constraints. This article details the primary methods game developers use to maintain visual fidelity, prevent motion sickness, avoid edge distortion, and ensure competitive fairness across diverse ultrawide hardware setups.

Horizontal+ (Hor+) Aspect Ratio Scaling

The industry standard for handling ultrawide resolutions is Horizontal+ (Hor+) scaling. Rather than cropping the top and bottom of the frame to fit wider aspect ratios (Vert- scaling, which reduces usable screen space), Hor+ locks the vertical field of view and expands the horizontal field of view automatically based on the monitor's aspect ratio.

By calculating the horizontal FOV dynamically:

\[\text{Horizontal FOV} = 2 \times \arctan\left(\tan\left(\frac{\text{Vertical FOV}}{2}\right) \times \text{Aspect Ratio}\right)\]

This formula ensures that players maintain the intended vertical context of the game world while naturally gaining peripheral vision on wider displays.

Independent Viewmodel and World Camera Systems

In first-person games, expanding the world FOV for ultrawide screens often distorts the player's hands, weapons, or cockpit models (viewmodels). To solve this, developers decouple the rendering of the world camera from the viewmodel camera:

  • World Camera: Uses dynamic Hor+ scaling to provide an expansive view of the environment.
  • Viewmodel Camera: Uses a fixed FOV and separate projection matrix to prevent weapons from stretching, exposing unmodeled geometry, or clipping through the screen boundaries.

Non-Rectilinear Projections for Extreme Aspect Ratios

Standard game engines use rectilinear (pinhole) projection, which keeps straight lines straight. However, on super-ultrawide displays (such as 32:9), high horizontal FOV values cause severe peripheral stretching and distortion (the "fisheye" effect at the screen edges).

To mitigate this, developers use advanced projection methods:

  • Pannini (Panini) Projection: Compresses peripheral areas horizontally while preserving vertical lines, reducing perceived distortion at the edges of wide screens.
  • Cylindrical Projection: Maps the scene onto a cylinder rather than a flat plane, softening distortion on multi-monitor or curved ultrawide setups.
  • Hybrid Dynamic Projection: Blends rectilinear projection in the center of the screen with cylindrical or Pannini projections toward the outer edges.

Adaptive FOV Clamping and UI Anchoring

Allowing arbitrary FOV adjustments can break game mechanics, introduce rendering artifacts, or cause performance drops due to aggressive frustum culling limits. Developers balance this by implementing dynamic clamp ranges in the game's settings menu.

  • Relative FOV Sliders: Presenting FOV sliders based on horizontal degrees calculated for the current aspect ratio, or exposing vertical FOV directly to ensure cross-ratio consistency.
  • HUD and UI Anchoring: Decoupling the HUD from the viewport edges, allowing players to constrain UI elements to a central 16:9 or 21:9 boundary so critical information remains within their central line of sight.

Competitive Balancing Constraints

In multiplayer and esports titles, expanded FOV on ultrawide monitors can provide an unfair tactical advantage by granting wider peripheral awareness. Developers use two main strategies to balance competitive play:

  • Fixed FOV with Letterboxing/Pillarboxing: Restricting the rendered gameplay viewport to a standard 16:9 ratio during competitive matches, rendering black bars on the sides while keeping menus or static HUD elements in the ultrawide space.
  • Aggressive Culling and Angle Caps: Capping the maximum allowable horizontal angle of view and culling enemy player models or high-detail geometry outside a standard visibility cone, preventing ultrawide users from gaining early visual cues on opponents.
