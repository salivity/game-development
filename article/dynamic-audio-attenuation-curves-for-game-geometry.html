Dynamic Audio Attenuation Curves for Game Geometry

In modern game development, realistic audio relies on sound dynamically adapting to the player's physical environment. Audio engineers achieve this by combining distance-based attenuation curves with real-time acoustic simulation techniques such as ray tracing, occlusion modeling, and spatial audio middleware. By evaluating the surrounding room geometry, surface materials, and line-of-sight paths, game engines dynamically modify attenuation shapes, frequency filtering, and reverb properties to create believable three-dimensional soundscapes.

Fundamentals of Attenuation Curves

Sound attenuation defines how audio volume decreases as the distance between the sound emitter and the listener increases. Traditional implementations rely on static mathematical curves:

  • Logarithmic/Inverse Square Curves: Mimic real-world physics, where sound energy drops significantly over short distances and levels off over longer ranges.
  • Linear Curves: Offer predictable, gameplay-friendly volume drop-offs often used for non-diegetic or critical gameplay cues.
  • Custom S-Curves: Allow designers to preserve vocal clarity at mid-range while dropping off sharply past a designated threshold.

In dynamic environments, static curves are insufficient. Audio engineers must adjust curve parameters—such as the minimum radius, maximum distance, and roll-off steepness—in real time depending on whether the sound is enclosed, open, or partially blocked.

Real-Time Geometry Analysis and Ray Tracing

To adapt attenuation to room geometry, modern game engines use physics queries and audio ray tracing (or acoustic pathfinding):

  1. Ray Casting: The engine shoots multiple rays from the sound source toward the listener and surrounding surfaces to detect walls, ceilings, and openings (portals).
  2. Volumetric Probes: Pre-baked or dynamic acoustic probes sample the room's volume and surface areas to determine the acoustic signature of the space.
  3. Path Length Calculation: If a direct path is blocked, the engine calculates the indirect path sound takes around corners or through doorways, dynamically extending the effective distance value fed into the attenuation curve.

Occlusion, Obstruction, and Diffraction

Room geometry fundamentally changes sound transmission through three primary phenomena:

  • Occlusion: Occurs when an emitter is completely enclosed in a separate room. The sound must pass through physical geometry, causing massive attenuation and heavy low-pass filtering.
  • Obstruction: Occurs when a sound source and listener share the same room, but a specific object (like a pillar) blocks the direct line of sight. The direct sound is attenuated, while the room's early reflections and reverberation remain largely unaffected.
  • Diffraction: Simulates sound bending around edges and doorframes. The engine modifies the perceived origin of the sound to match the opening, adjusting the attenuation curve to treat the doorway as a secondary virtual emitter.

Acoustic Material Properties and Dynamic Filtering

Surrounding room geometry is not defined by shape alone; material composition directly shapes attenuation behavior:

  • Absorption Coefficients: Hard materials (concrete, glass) reflect high-frequency sound, while soft materials (wood, fabric, carpet) absorb it. Audio engineers map material tags to specific low-pass and high-pass filter values.
  • Dynamic Curve Shifting: In a tight, reflective corridor, the attenuation curve may be flattened to simulate sound funneling and maintaining intensity over greater distances. In open terrain or heavily furnished rooms, the curve steepens to simulate rapid sound dissipation.

Implementation via Audio Middleware

Audio engineers implement these systems using middleware such as Audiokinetic Wwise, FMOD, or spatial audio plugins like Steam Audio and Microsoft Project Acoustics.

Engineers configure Remote Procedure Calls (RPCs) and Game-Defined Parameters (RTPCs) that bridge the game’s physics engine with the audio engine. As the player traverses different rooms, the game pushes runtime data—such as room volume, occlusion percentage, and transmission loss—to modulate the active attenuation curves, low-pass filter cutoffs, and wet/dry reverb balances seamlessly.
