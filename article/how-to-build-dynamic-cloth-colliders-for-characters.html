How to Build Dynamic Cloth Colliders for Characters

In real-time game development, technical artists prevent dynamic cloth meshes from clipping through character limbs by building precise skeletal-driven collider networks. By utilizing primitive collision shapes—primarily dynamic capsules and spheres—bound to character joint hierarchies, artists approximate underlying anatomy without incurring the high computational cost of full mesh-to-mesh collision. This article covers the essential workflow for creating stable dynamic cloth colliders, including collision shape selection, bone parenting, margin management, joint pinch handling, and engine-level physics optimization.

1. Choosing the Right Collision Primitives

Mesh-to-mesh collision is generally too expensive for real-time dynamic cloth simulation. Instead, technical artists approximate character anatomy using analytical primitives:

  • Capsules: The industry standard for limbs (arms, thighs, calves). Capsules provide smooth, continuous surface math that allows simulated cloth vertices to glide without getting caught on sharp angles.
  • Tapered Capsules: Advanced cloth solvers (such as Unreal Engine's Chaos Cloth) support tapered or dual-radius capsules, allowing the collider to match the natural narrowing of a forearm or calf.
  • Spheres: Used primarily for joints with complex rotations (shoulders, hips) or isolated regions like the head and knuckles.
  • Plane/Convex Hulls: Rarely used for limbs, but occasionally applied as limiters to prevent cloth from entering torso cavities.

2. Rig Integration and Hierarchy Binding

Colliders must accurately follow skeletal deformation during animations:

  • Direct Bone Parenting: Colliders are bound directly to the corresponding deformation bones (e.g., upperarm_l, lowerarm_l). As the animation rig moves, the transform of the collision primitive updates automatically.
  • Skeletal Offsets: Technical artists align the centerline of each capsule with the primary axis of the bone, manually adjusting the radius so the collider sits just below or flush with the visible character mesh.
  • Multi-Bone Blending: For soft tissue regions (such as the chest or buttocks) that deform via twist bones or secondary physics, colliders are parented to helper joints or driven via driven keys/constraints rather than main deformation bones.

3. Managing Collision Margins and Offsets

Preventing visual penetration requires balancing collider size with solver margins:

  • Virtual Thickness / Contact Offsets: Cloth solvers assign a thickness parameter to both the cloth fabric and the colliders. If a capsule matches the exact visual boundary of a limb, setting a small positive contact offset (e.g., 0.5 cm to 1.5 cm) provides a cushion that prevents cloth vertices from resting beneath the rendered skin surface.
  • Over-Sizing vs. Visual Hovering: Making a collider too large prevents clipping but causes clothing to noticeably "hover" over the skin. Technical artists fine-tune the collider radius alongside the cloth mesh's friction parameters to maintain contact realism without penetration.

4. Handling Joint Pinching and Articulation Gaps

Extreme joint flexion (such as a fully bent knee or elbow) creates geometric pinch points where cloth is easily trapped or forced through the mesh:

  • Linked Capsule Endpoints: Modern solvers allow the end sphere of a parent capsule (upper arm) to link directly to the start sphere of a child capsule (forearm). This forms a continuous, unbroken volume at the elbow joint regardless of rotation angle.
  • Joint Overlap Smoothing: Where linked capsules are unavailable, artists overlap the joint-centered spheres slightly. This ensures no dynamic gap opens during high-angle bends.
  • Backface Culling & Push-Out Constraints: Backstop constraints (position limits relative to the skinned mesh) are painted onto cloth vertices. If high-velocity animation forces a vertex past the dynamic capsule, the backstop constraint pushes the vertex back out toward the surface normal.

5. Solver Settings and Performance Optimization

Even well-placed colliders fail if the physics solver does not evaluate frequently enough:

  • Substepping / Iterations: High-velocity movements (such as weapon swings or martial arts kicks) require increased solver substeps. Substepping evaluates collisions multiple times per frame, preventing fast-moving limbs from tunneling through cloth.
  • Continuous Collision Detection (CCD): Enabling CCD on critical colliders prevents tunneling at the cost of a slight performance hit.
  • LOD-Based Collider Stripping: For background characters or distant Levels of Detail (LODs), technical artists disable finger, foot, and secondary colliders, retaining only core torso and limb primitives to preserve frame rates.
