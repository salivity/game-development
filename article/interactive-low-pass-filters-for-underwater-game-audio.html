Interactive Low-Pass Filters for Underwater Game Audio

In aquatic game development, simulating the muffled acoustics of being submerged requires dynamic audio processing driven by in-game events. Audio engineers achieve this primarily through interactive low-pass filters (LPFs) controlled via game engines and audio middleware. By dynamically attenuating high-frequency content as the player enters the water, blending contextual audio assets, and smoothing the transition through parameter interpolation, developers create an authentic and immersive underwater acoustic environment.

The Physics and the Low-Pass Filter

When a human listener is submerged in water, the acoustic impedance mismatch between water and air inside the ear canal—coupled with bone conduction bypassing the eardrum—causes a drastic reduction in perceived high frequencies. A low-pass filter mimics this phenomenon by allowing low frequencies below a designated cutoff threshold to pass through unaffected while attenuating frequencies above that cutoff. In game development, an interactive LPF typically sweeps the cutoff frequency from the standard audible ceiling (roughly 20 kHz) down to a range between 200 Hz and 800 Hz to produce the characteristic muffled sensation.

Tracking Submersion with Game Engine Logic

Before audio DSP (digital signal processing) can be applied, the game engine must detect the exact moment and depth of submersion. This is typically handled through:

  • Listener-Based Collision: The audio listener component (usually attached to the player’s camera) checks for overlap with designated water volumes.
  • Surface Height Checks: The engine calculates the delta between the camera's Z-axis position and the water mesh's procedural surface height, generating a normalized floating-point value (0.0 for fully surfaced, 1.0 for fully submerged).
  • Ear-Level Raycasts: Precise systems cast rays from the virtual ear positions to account for partial submersion, enabling dynamic half-in, half-out states.

Routing and Middleware Implementation

Modern game development commonly relies on audio middleware such as Audiokinetic Wwise or FMOD, as well as native engine submix systems like Unreal Engine’s Sound Submixes or Unity’s Audio Mixer.

Engineers route environmental audio, Foley, and voice tracks to a common master or auxiliary bus. Rather than applying filters to individual sound clips, an LPF plugin is placed directly on the destination bus as an insert effect. Game engines expose the submersion depth via a Real-Time Parameter Control (RTPC) or a game parameter exposed to code. As the parameter changes, the middleware dynamically shifts the filter's cutoff frequency, resonance (Q factor), and overall bus gain.

Smoothing and Interpolation

Instantaneous filter changes produce audible clicks, pops, and unnatural auditory jarring. To prevent this, audio engineers apply interpolation techniques:

  • Slew Rates and Smoothing Times: A transition time—typically between 100 to 300 milliseconds—is applied to the parameter shift, ensuring a continuous sweep even if the player drops into the water abruptly.
  • Non-Linear Curves: S-curves or logarithmic curves are favored over linear sweeps to emulate human auditory perception, matching the logarithmic nature of both frequency perception and fluid resistance.

Complementary Processing Beyond the Filter

A low-pass filter alone is rarely sufficient to sell the illusion of submersion. Audio engineers combine the interactive LPF with complementary design techniques:

  • Dynamic Ducking: Non-essential audio stems (such as distant gunshots or wind) are sidechained and attenuated, reducing acoustic clutter.
  • Hydrophone and Bubble Layering: Entering the water triggers a dedicated underwater sub-bass drone, muffled bubbling, and interior body sounds (such as a heartbeat or breathing apparatus).
  • Reverb and Resonance Shifts: Underwater sound travels roughly four times faster than in air, shortening perceived reflections. Engineers shorten early reflection delays while introducing a slightly metallic, resonant low-frequency reverb tail to simulate aquatic density.
