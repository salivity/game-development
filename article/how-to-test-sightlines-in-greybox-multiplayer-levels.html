How to Test Sightlines in Greybox Multiplayer Levels

Testing sightline readability during the initial greybox phase ensures that competitive multiplayer arenas remain fair, readable, and strategically balanced before visual art is applied. Designers evaluate sightlines using scale mannequins, camera-level audits, debug raycasting tools, and structured untextured playtests to spot visual clutter, unfair peek angles, and ambiguous engagement distances. By isolating geometric visibility from lighting and texture work early, level designers establish clear visual hierarchy and predictable combat ranges.

Mannequin Placement and Scale Verification

Before running live tests, designers populate key map locations with static player mannequins. These dummy meshes represent standard standing, crouching, and prone player heights.

  • Anchor Positions: Mannequins are placed at primary sniper nests, common anchor spots, and bomb sites or capture zones.
  • Head-Glitch Audits: Designers approach cover to verify how much of the mannequin's silhouette is exposed. Cover geometry is adjusted to prevent "head-glitching," where a player can shoot over an obstacle while exposing only a fraction of their forehead.
  • Silhouette Separation: Mannequins are viewed against standard backdrops from maximum engagement distances to ensure their shapes break the horizon cleanly rather than blending into adjacent geometry.

Value Contrast and Color Blocking

Greyboxing is rarely monochromatic. Designers use a deliberate range of grey values and basic flat colors to test visual contrast.

  • Three-Tone Value System: Surfaces are assigned values based on priority: floors are often mid-grey, low cover is dark grey, and critical backdrops behind choke points are light grey. This value separation forces player models to stand out clearly against background surfaces.
  • Hazard and Objective Highlighting: Highly saturated accent colors (such as orange or cyan) are reserved strictly for objective areas, interactive geometry, and high-danger threshold markers to keep background noise from competing with targets.
  • Visual Noise Elimination: Designers remove unnecessary geometric bevels, complex primitive intersections, and repetitive tiling that could cause optical illusions or camouflage player movement during motion.

Engine Debug Tools and Line-of-Sight Raycasting

Modern game engines provide procedural and debug tools to mathematically test visibility across a greybox layout.

  • Raycast Sweeps: Designers use script-based raycasts between spawns, choke points, and objectives to automatically detect open visibility paths that violate map timing rules.
  • Vision Cones and Frustum Volumes: By attaching persistent camera frustum visualizations to key defensive power positions, designers identify unintended cross-lane sightlines that could allow players to dominate multiple paths simultaneously.
  • Heatmap Analysis: Automated bots or early playtesters generate line-of-sight exposure heatmaps, highlighting zones where players are visible from too many angles at once.

Playtesting Without Minimaps and UI

The definitive test for sightline readability is active playtesting with in-game assists stripped away.

  • HUD-Free Testing: Designers disable the minimap, overhead player nameplates, and hit indicators. If players cannot acquire, identify, and track targets at intended combat ranges using pure visual recognition, the level geometry requires adjustment.
  • First-Look Sweeps: Testers enter lanes from standard spawn approaches to monitor how many potential threat angles appear on screen simultaneously. The standard rule is the "Rule of Two": a player should rarely be exposed to more than two contested sightlines at any single transition point.
  • Pixel-Peek Checks: Testers deliberately search for sub-meter gaps between modular blocks. These narrow visual slits give defenders unfair, risk-free vision down entire lanes and are sealed during geometry passes.

Architectural Framing and Occlusion

Geometry is actively shaped to guide player attention toward critical threat areas while occluding irrelevant space.

  • Framing Apertures: Doorways, arches, and structural overhangs act as natural view limiters, restricting how much of an open area a player can see at one time.
  • Depth Layering: Designers break long, flat sightlines by placing staggered, non-continuous cover blocks. This creates distinct visual depth bands (foreground, midground, background) that make target distance estimation instantaneous during high-speed engagements.
