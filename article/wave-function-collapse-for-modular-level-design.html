Wave Function Collapse for Modular Level Design

The Wave Function Collapse (WFC) algorithm synthesizes complex, rule-abiding modular level layouts by treating procedural generation as a constraint satisfaction problem. Inspired by quantum mechanics and constraint-propagation techniques in computer science, WFC initializes an environment where every tile or voxel exists in a superposition of all possible states. By iteratively collapsing these possibilities based on strictly defined adjacency rules and propagating the consequences across a grid, the algorithm guarantees seamless, cohesive environments such as dungeons, road networks, and architectural complexes without requiring hand-crafted placement.

The Foundation: Superposition and Adjacency Rules

The algorithm begins by dividing a target space into an array of discrete cells, such as a 2D grid, a 3D voxel volume, or a topological graph. In the initial state, every cell is in "superposition," meaning it simultaneously holds every candidate module from a predefined tileset.

Alongside the tileset, developers define compatibility constraints. These constraints dictate which modules can legally touch along their corresponding edges, faces, or sockets. Compatibility can be determined in two ways:

  • The Tiled Model: Designers explicitly declare valid socket pairings (e.g., a "road-north" socket can only connect to a "road-south" socket).
  • The Overlapping Model: The algorithm automatically extracts adjacency relationships by scanning an input sample image or 3D blockout and indexing every recurring pattern.

Step 1: Entropy Evaluation

To decide where to build next, WFC evaluates the "entropy" of each cell in the layout. In this context, Shannon entropy measures the degree of uncertainty in a cell based on how many valid module choices remain, often weighted by the intended frequency of those modules.

The algorithm searches the entire space for uncollapsed cells with the lowest non-zero entropy. Choosing the cell with the fewest valid options minimizes the risk of generating an impossible configuration later, as it addresses the most constrained parts of the map first.

Step 2: Observation and Collapse

Once the cell with the lowest entropy is selected, the algorithm "observes" or collapses it. Out of the remaining valid modules for that cell, one is chosen at random, typically using designer-specified weights to bias the frequency of certain architectural elements over others. The chosen cell is now fixed to a single definitive state, dropping its entropy to zero.

Step 3: Constraint Propagation

Collapsing a single cell alters the valid states of its immediate neighbors. If a neighbor previously had five possible states, the newly collapsed cell may invalidate two of them because they violate adjacency rules.

WFC uses a constraint satisfaction method similar to the AC-3 (Arc Consistency 3) algorithm:

  1. The collapsed cell removes incompatible states from its adjacent neighbors.
  2. If a neighbor loses one or more valid options, its own neighbors must be checked to see if their options have also been invalidated.
  3. This process continues recursively in a cascade, pruning possibilities outward through the grid until the system reaches a stable state where no further invalid options exist.

Step 4: Iteration and Contradiction Handling

The algorithm repeats the cycle—find the lowest entropy cell, collapse it, and propagate constraints—until every cell contains exactly one module.

Occasionally, propagation can strip a cell of all possible choices, creating a contradiction where no module can legally fit. Procedural pipelines handle contradictions in two ways:

  • Restarting: For smaller or mid-sized grids, simply discarding the failed attempt and restarting the process is computationally cheap and often unnoticeable.
  • Backtracking: For larger, continuous worlds, the algorithm can revert a fixed number of steps, restore the previous superpositions, and attempt an alternative collapse path to resolve the local conflict.

Why WFC Excels in Level Generation

WFC bridges the gap between purely random generation and rigid deterministic systems. Because it evaluates local constraints rather than global templates, it can organically fit intricate modular pieces—such as multi-story buildings, staircase connections, and intersecting piping—into irregular footprints. The resulting levels maintain the structural integrity and aesthetic cohesion of hand-crafted environments while providing the infinite variability required for procedural games.
