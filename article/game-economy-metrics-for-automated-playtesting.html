Game Economy Metrics for Automated Playtesting

Automated continuous playtesting bots allow game developers to simulate thousands of gameplay hours in minutes, exposing economic exploits, hyperinflation, and progression deadlocks before launch. To effectively stress-test an in-game economy, developers must monitor a specific set of quantitative metrics that capture resource generation, consumption, circulation, and player purchasing power across diverse playstyles.

1. Faucet-to-Sink Ratios (Net Inflow vs. Outflow)

The faucet-to-sink ratio measures the total amount of currency or resources generated (faucets) against the amount permanently removed from the game (sinks). Bots should test this across various progression milestones. A healthy economy maintains an equilibrium where sinks scale dynamically with faucets. If the ratio exceeds 1.0 significantly over time, the economy risks hyperinflation; if it drops far below 1.0, players face currency starvation that halts progression.

2. Time-to-Acquisition (TTA)

Time-to-Acquisition tracks the active play duration required for a bot to earn specific items, upgrades, or unlocks. By running different bot archetypes—such as optimal min-maxers, casual wanderers, and low-skill players—developers can identify whether item costs create excessive grinds or allow players to skip progression tiers too quickly. Large discrepancies in TTA between bot archetypes often signal balancing issues in reward payouts.

3. Gini Coefficient of Wealth Distribution

Applying the Gini coefficient to bot populations measures the inequality of currency and asset distribution over time. A coefficient approaching 1.0 indicates that a tiny fraction of top-tier playstyles or exploits hoard all wealth, while lower-performing bots remain impoverished. Monitoring this metric helps prevent systemic balance issues where high-efficiency strategies trivialize economic challenges while average players struggle.

4. Currency Velocity and Hoarding Rates

Currency velocity measures how frequently units of currency change hands or get reinvested into gameplay systems versus sitting idle in inventories. High hoarding rates indicate a lack of compelling late-game sinks or fear of scarcity, while excessively high velocity can signal that players immediately discard currency due to a lack of meaningful long-term savings goals.

5. Progression Bottleneck and Chokepoint Frequency

Tracking resource-induced failure rates pinpoints exact moments where bots run out of essential consumables, crafting materials, or currency needed to progress. If automated agents repeatedly stall at specific levels or questlines due to economic deficits, it indicates a miscalibrated economy gate rather than a skill gate.

6. Secondary Market Price Volatility

For games featuring player-driven markets, auction houses, or trading systems, bots trading among themselves provide crucial data on supply, demand, and price stability. Rapid price spikes in specific commodities indicate artificial shortages or runaway generation of raw materials, exposing vulnerabilities to market cornering and speculative trading.
