Trademark Protection for Indie Games Before Open Beta

Launching an open beta exposes a video game to the public, creating immediate intellectual property vulnerabilities if proper legal safeguards are not established in advance. This article details the essential trademark protection steps an independent game studio must implement prior to an open beta launch, covering trademark clearance searches, filing intent-to-use applications, securing key digital identifiers, and enforcing brand rights within the game build and public community channels.

1. Conduct a Comprehensive Trademark Clearance Search

Before announcing or releasing a beta build, verify that the game's title, studio name, and distinct branding do not infringe on existing trademarks.

  • Search Official Trademark Databases: Search the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) database, the European Union Intellectual Property Office (EUIPO), the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO), and the relevant registries in your target markets.
  • Search Common Law and Industry Channels: Search Steam, itch.io, Google Play, Apple App Store, Epic Games Store, and social media platforms. Unregistered games still retain "common law" trademark rights in many jurisdictions, which can prevent you from using your chosen title.

2. File Trademark Applications in the Correct Classes

Secure formal trademark registrations early. If the game is not yet generating revenue, file an Intent-to-Use (ITU) application (such as a Section 1(b) filing in the United States) to establish an early priority date before the public beta goes live.

Register under the relevant international trademark classes:

  • Class 9: Downloadable computer game software, video game programs, and mobile applications.
  • Class 41: Providing online non-downloadable video games and online entertainment services.
  • Class 25 & Class 28 (Optional): Physical merchandise, clothing, and board games/toys if physical distribution is planned.

Prioritize trademarking the primary game title first, followed by the studio name and key graphical logos.

3. Secure All Digital Handles and Web Domains

Prevent cybersquatting and brand confusion by registering all relevant online real estate before the open beta announcement:

  • Top-level domain names (e.g., .com, .gg, studio-specific domains).
  • Official social media handles on platforms such as X (Twitter), YouTube, TikTok, Reddit, and Twitch.
  • Community infrastructure, including official Discord server vanity URLs and Steam community hub URLs.

4. Apply Trademark Notices in the Beta Build

Clearly indicate trademark ownership within the game client and marketing collateral to establish formal notice to the public:

  • Use the ™ Symbol: Use the "™" symbol next to the game title and studio name for pending or unregistered marks. Only use the "®" symbol once a trademark is officially registered with a national trademark office.
  • Add Splash Screen Notices: Include a legal notice on the title screen, splash screens, and credits (e.g., "Game Title™ is a trademark of Studio Name LLC. All rights reserved.").
  • Include IP Clauses in the Beta EULA: Require open beta participants to accept an End User License Agreement (EULA) and Terms of Service (ToS) that explicitly state all intellectual property, titles, logos, and in-game assets remain the exclusive property of the studio.

5. Establish Brand Guidelines for Content Creators

An open beta invites streaming, video creation, and community-generated content. Publish a clear Content Creator Policy on your official website that grants players a limited, revocable license to display gameplay while restricting the unauthorized commercial use or registration of your trademarks, logos, and game assets.
