Procedural Flora Scattering Algorithms in Games

Procedural flora scattering relies on a combination of spatial sampling, noise evaluation, and multi-factor mathematical constraints to realistically place vegetation across planetary surfaces. By evaluating digital elevation data—specifically slope steepness, elevation thresholds, and soil composition—developers use mathematical filters alongside distribution algorithms to populate vast terrains dynamically without manual artist intervention.

Environmental Constraint Filtering

Before scattering flora, the terrain must be evaluated to determine whether a given point is habitable for specific plant species. This relies on mathematical masks derived from the terrain's geometry and texture data:

  • Slope Angle Calculation (Surface Normal Evaluation): The local steepness of the terrain is evaluated by taking the dot product of the surface normal (\(\vec{N}\)) and the global up-vector (\(\vec{Up}\), which is the normalized vector pointing from the planet's center to the surface point). The resulting cosine value is mapped to an angle. Developers use smoothstep functions to establish minimum and maximum slope thresholds, preventing large trees from spawning on vertical cliffs while allowing cliff-dwelling shrubs to appear within narrow angular bands.
  • Altitude Mapping: Elevation is sampled directly from the planet's height field or distance from the planet core. Multi-band thresholding defines biological zones, such as sea-level marshes, temperate forests, and high-altitude alpine zones. A transition function feathers the boundaries between these bands to avoid harsh lines, simulating real-world tree lines where altitude prohibits tree growth.
  • Soil Type and Biome Splatting: Soil composition is typically represented via splat maps, Voronoi diagrams, or multi-frequency noise functions (such as Simplex noise combined with Fractional Brownian Motion). Each soil type (e.g., sand, rich loam, rock) is assigned a weight channel. The flora generation system queries this channel to match a plant's environmental affinity to the substrate.

Spatial Distribution Algorithms

Once the environmental viability of a terrain patch is calculated as a scalar density value (a product of slope, altitude, and soil masks), spatial distribution algorithms determine the exact placement and spacing of individual flora instances:

  • Poisson Disk Sampling: To prevent unnatural clustering and visual clipping, Poisson disk sampling generates random points that maintain a minimum distance from one another. In procedural generation, variable-density Poisson disk algorithms (often using Bridson’s algorithm or Wang tiles) dynamically scale the exclusion radius based on the environmental density mask—allowing dense clustering in fertile valleys and sparse spacing on rocky hills.
  • Stratified Jittered Grid Sampling: A performance-optimized alternative to Poisson disk sampling involves dividing the terrain into a regular grid, placing a point at the center of each cell, and applying a pseudo-random offset (jitter). Points falling on invalid slope, altitude, or soil parameters are discarded. While faster than Poisson sampling, it requires careful tuning of the jitter range to avoid visible grid artifacts.
  • Rule-Based Clustering (Parent-Child Distributions): Many systems use hierarchical scattering to simulate seed dispersal. "Parent" trees are placed using low-frequency noise or Poisson distributions, and smaller "child" plants (saplings, bushes, flowers) are scattered in clusters around the parents using Gaussian distributions. The dispersion radius and density of the children are modulated by the local soil moisture and slope stability.

Real-Time GPU-Driven Execution

Modern game engines offload planetary-scale flora distribution to the GPU using compute shaders:

  • Indirect Compute Dispatch: Compute shaders sample the planet’s procedural height and biome generation pipeline in real time. The shader evaluates slope, altitude, and soil layers per candidate instance, culls points outside the camera's view frustum, and appends valid transformation matrices directly to a GPU buffer.
  • Hierarchical Chunking (Clipmaps and Quadtrees): Planets are split into level-of-detail (LOD) quadtree nodes. Flora algorithms are evaluated on-demand as the player approaches specific surface patches, ensuring memory is only allocated to active instances while distant terrain displays simplified billboard representations or bare ground textures.
